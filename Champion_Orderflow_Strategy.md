## 1. Executive Summary

Chris Creamer is one of the youngest World Cup day-trading champions ever. At 26, he competed in the Robbins World Cup (Micro Day Trading Championship) against full-time professionals and veterans, and finished July with a **100% return**.

In this episode he breaks down, live and in person for the first time, the full process he used to win — from the very first thing he checks before the market opens, to the exact moment he enters and exits a trade, and finally the psychological process that eliminated the bad trades destroying his results.

The core thesis:

- A trading **entry model or setup is only the final 5–10%** of the job. The real edge is **context**.
- The market is an **auction** between buyers and sellers. The goal is to find where one side is **forced to participate**, and then to trade the **failure** of that participation (trap participants).
- **Money is the byproduct of proper execution.** Focus on execution, not P&L.
- His strategy is deliberately **simple and repeatable** — three components: **Context → Location → Confirmation.**
- **Consistency comes from back-end optimization** (removing bad losses), not front-end optimization (chasing more setups).

## 2. The Four-Step Framework

Chris reduces his entire process into four stages:

1. **Environment** (Context) — What is the higher-timeframe market structure, and what is the volatility/gamma regime?
2. **Location** — *Where* do I want to do business? (Discount vs. premium, value area, Fibonacci golden pocket)
3. **Confirmation** — *Is this level real?* (Order flow, absorption, delta, shift of dominance)
4. **Trade Management** — Stop placement, targets, trailing, and reading effort vs. result in real time.

His framing: *"Context, location, then confirmation."*

## 3. Environment (Step 1 — Before the Open)

Everything in this stage is prepared **before the market opens**, because he refuses to make these decisions while price is moving fast. He wants scenarios built ahead of time.

### 3.1 Market Structure (Higher Timeframes)

- Reviewed on the **1-hour / 4-hour** charts.
- Purpose: understand what the **week** and **previous week** have been doing.
- He categorizes the environment as:
  - **Value up structure** (higher highs / higher lows)
  - **Value down structure** (lower highs / lower lows)
  - **Sideways / balanced**

He does **not** try to call tops or bottoms. In a value-up structure, he does **not** want to sell premium — he waits for a pullback into discount and trades in the direction of the structure.

### 3.2 Gamma Regime (GEX) — Volatility Context

**GEX = Gamma Exposure.** The options market is one of the largest markets; dealers and market makers who transact there do **not** want directional exposure, so they hedge in the underlying (futures). That hedging produces predictable flows.

| Regime | Dealer behavior | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Positive Gamma** (often green) | Sell into rips, buy into dips | **Volatility dampening** — harder to slice through; breakout attempts often **fail** |
| **Negative Gamma** (often red) | Buy into rips, sell into dips | **Volatility amplifier** — bigger, faster moves |

**Key misconception he corrects:** Positive gamma does **not** mean "long" (even though it's green); negative gamma does **not** mean "short" (even though it's red). Think in terms of **volatility**, not direction.

He also wants to know three levels:
- The **call wall**
- The **put wall**
- The **gamma flip zone** (the "line in the sand" between positive and negative territory)

**Naive GEX vs. Inferred GEX:**
- **Inferred GEX** — models that try to add granularity to dealer positioning. Requires expensive CBOE data (~$300/month) and is mostly limited to SPX/ES.
- **Naive GEX** — broad assumptions. Chris is fine with this because he uses gamma only to understand the **environment/regime**, **not** to pick specific bounce levels.
- He uses a web-based platform (e.g., **Tanuki Trade**) and looks at **NQ** levels (QQQ, NDX — since CBOE doesn't provide the NQ data).

**Worked example:** *"We're in a value-up structure and a negative gamma environment → volatility may be amplified; we may see bigger, faster moves."* He is *not* predicting direction — he is preparing for the type of day.

> Why it matters: the **killer of most retail accounts is a choppy environment.** People love to trade breakouts, but in positive gamma, breakouts tend to **fail** because dealers sell the rips.

## 4. Location (Step 2 — Where to Do Business)

"Location" is not just a box on the chart — it answers: **where do I actually want to do business?**

### 4.1 Discount vs. Premium (Volume / Value Area)

- Measured on **daily / cash session profiles** (Mon, Tue, Wed…).
- **Below value area = discount** → where he wants to buy in a value-up structure.
- **Above value area = premium** → an "expensive" place to get involved.
- Concept: don't chase extension up high; wait for a **pullback into discount**.

### 4.2 Inefficiency & Low-Volume Nodes

- If a move happened **quickly** (e.g., during Asia session), it may be an **inefficient** part of the move — price didn't spend enough time there to conduct business.
- **Low-volume nodes** (little business transacted) are areas the market may later revisit.

### 4.3 The Fibonacci "Golden Pocket" (His Discount Zone)

He defines the discount zone using **Fibonacci retracement** from **swing low → swing high**, marking:

- **0.705**
- **0.788**
- **0.886**

These create a **zone** (a "golden pocket" idea). Rules for the zone to be valid:

1. The fib zone must be **outside of value area** (if it sits inside value area, he doesn't want it).
2. He wants to see **internal structure** going into the move — essentially just a clean **swing point**; the market breathes with pullbacks.

**The 0.886 level is critical:** it's the final area where his pullback-to-continuation trade should occur. If buyers **cannot shift dominance back to the upside** and price goes **below the 0.886**, the trade is **invalidated** — he does not take it.

## 5. Confirmation (Step 3 — Order Flow & Entry)

Once price is in location (discount), the question becomes: **is this level real?** *"Just because I draw a box on the chart doesn't mean price will respect it."*

### 5.1 Footprint / Order Flow Candles

He trades **5-minute charts** (using 1H and 15M for context, sometimes 1M). He typically uses **two types of candles**:

- A candle with a **volume profile** inside it
- A candle with a **delta profile** inside it

A normal candlestick shows **open, high, low, close** — the *result*, the "scoreboard." The footprint shows **how** it formed — the bid/ask, the ladder of volume and delta at each price.

### 5.2 Absorption

**Absorption** = aggressive participants pushing in one direction but **not getting rewarded** with price progression.

- He wants to see it **at the extremes** — in the **wicks** of the candle.
- Example: a candle pushes down aggressively with a **large lower wick**, and **all the volume / POC / negative delta** is concentrated at the bottom. Sellers are piling in at the extreme but failing to push price lower.
- **Important caveat:** absorption does **not** mean automatic reversal. It happens constantly. What he needs is the **shift of dominance** back to the other side.

### 5.3 Shift of Dominance (The Trigger)

His sequence for a long entry:

1. Candle flips **bullish** after absorption in discount.
2. Next candle opens and **immediately pulls back**.
3. He wants sellers to **try again** — and to **fail higher up** (failure of sellers at a higher price).
4. When that fails and price flips bullish again → **he goes long.**

This "try, fail, try again, fail" structure is his core confirmation of a **regime/dominance shift**.

**Imbalance tooling:** his indicator lights up footprint numbers in **bold** when there's an **imbalance of 400% or more** (on the ask, passive orders are filled by aggressive buyers; on the bid, by aggressive sellers). This visually flags **real aggression coming back the other way**.

- Right side = aggressive buyers · Left side = aggressive sellers
- He still **waits** — whipsaws happen. He wants the **second failure** before entering.

## 6. Entry, Stop Loss & Targets

### 6.1 Entry

- On the **second failure** of the counter-side and the flip back in his direction.
- Optionally confirmed by **buy-side imbalances** lifting the offer aggressively.

### 6.2 Stop Loss

- Placed **on the other side of the failed sellers** (below the seller-failure area).
- Logic: the idea is invalidated the moment sellers push past the area they **previously could not push past**.

### 6.3 Targets

- **Primary target: swing points** (prior highs/lows). *"If the market never takes a swing point, we'd just flatline — that's not what the market does."*
- Secondary references: **POC**, **call wall**, **psychological levels**, and **clustered orders** on the book / Level 2.
- **Trailing:** as price approaches a cluster (POC, call wall, etc.), he may **trail his stop** even if his full target is higher.
- Typical realized outcome: **~1.5R to 2R**. He stresses he doesn't need huge R multiples.

### 6.4 The Squeeze Logic (Why Moves Can Be Fast)

When traders who shorted in discount are forced to cover (buy back), and they're **off-sides**, they **accelerate** the move. In a **negative gamma** environment you also have dealers **buying the rip** — so the squeeze can be **very fast**.

## 7. Trade Management (Real-Time)

While in the trade, he watches the **footprint candles (bid×ask volume + delta)** to verify:

> **Effort vs. Result.** Is the aggression producing actual price progression?

- If buyers keep pushing and price keeps progressing → he **trails behind that aggression**.
- If buyers show **lots of aggression but no progression** (the inverse of the entry pattern) → **red flag**. He may **move to break-even** or **cut the trade**.
- Similarly, on the way back into value area: if buyers step in aggressively but **can't reclaim value area**, he tends to break-even or exit; if they **do** reclaim value area, he can target POC or a swing high.

## 8. Filters & Risk Rules

### 8.1 Participation Threshold (Hard Filter)

- He trades **MNQ** and looks at **MNQ order flow** (he notes some prefer NQ; this works for him).
- **Threshold: 20,000 contracts per 5-minute candle.**
- Below that, **participation is dying out** (e.g., heading into lunch) → **he doesn't touch it.**
- This is a big reason he only trades roughly the **first ~1.5 hours of the NY open** (sometimes Asia). He looks at charts constantly but only *participates* when there's heavy volume.

### 8.2 Performance Metrics

| Typical R multiple | **~1.5R–2R** |

### 8.3 Why 1.5R Is Enough

His framing around prop firms: a **$2,000 drawdown** on a $50K account vs. a **$3,000 profit target** is literally a **1.5R** requirement. Risking only 1% ($20 on a smaller account) is unrealistic given the drawdown rules — so you must risk more, which makes a consistent **1.5R** a sensible, survivable target.

### 8.4 The Psychology of R

- Most traders over-focus on huge R (10R, 20R) and brag about tiny stops and "bottom-tick / top-tick" entries.
- His stance: *"I don't care about looking cool. I want to be right."* If extra confirmation ruins the theoretical bottom tick but makes him **right more often**, he takes it.
- For beginners, chasing big R is **mentally brutal** (many small losses) → frustration → random behavior → blown account. That's "basically the majority of prop firm traders who never pull payouts."

## 9. Psychology: Good Losses vs. Bad Losses & the A/B/C Game

### 9.1 A-Game, B-Game, C-Game Sessions

- These grades have **nothing to do with P&L** — they measure **how well you executed.**
- **Consistency does not come from more A-game sessions** (you don't control how many A+ setups you get). It comes from **eliminating the back end** — the C-game sessions.
- **Back-end optimization > front-end optimization.**
- The hell loop: pass an eval → get funded → blow the account on day four, all because of one **C-game session**.

### 9.2 Good Loss vs. Bad Loss

- **Good loss:** You executed exactly as planned (no hesitation, no chasing, no FOMO, no early entry) and the trade still lost. That's **variance** in a probabilistic environment — a **cost of doing business**.
- **Bad loss:** You **bent your rules** — anticipated instead of waited for confirmation; entered without seeing sellers absorbed / dominance shift. Even if it *wins*, it's a **bad trade** because it **reinforces bad behavior**, which creates C-game sessions.

### 9.3 Rules Must Be Specific

- Rules like *"don't overtrade"* or *"don't oversize"* are **not rules**. Real rules attach a **specific action**, and they must be **specific to you**.
- Every trader has a **"line in the sand"** — the point where they shift from calculated decisions to emotional ones. It's different for everyone (a dollar P&L amount, a number of break-evens, a number of losses, or even **overconfidence** when winning).
- **Diagnosis is hard** because it's often invisible to the person it's happening to. Traders usually point at the *big losing trade*, but that's not where it broke — some earlier **sequence of events** did.
- **Once you find it, build a solution in front of it.**

### 9.4 Chris's Personal Risk Rules

- **Hard shut-off time** (~1.5 hrs into the open): his data showed his trades degraded dramatically after that point (less focus / trying to force something after missing the morning move).
- **Stop after two losses in a row** (not three): he noticed that after three losses there was ~a 50/50 chance he'd start making bad decisions — so he stops one step earlier.
- **One mistake → shut it down.** If he enters prematurely, chases, or assumes instead of watches, he stops for the day.

- Result: when you focus on proper execution, **money becomes the byproduct** — and then it's just **repetition**, plus **adjusting to regime/volatility changes.**

## 11. Advice for Struggling Traders

- Stop believing it's an **information problem** — it's usually a **behavior problem.**
- Some people genuinely aren't suited to trading because their behavior doesn't complement it.
- **You are not alone** — most traders struggle; stop comparing yourself to the winning posts on the internet and **focus on yourself.**
- If you're struggling: **size all the way down** for a month — down to a size that feels *almost insulting* — so you can focus purely on **executing properly** and building the **right habits.**
- For beginners who want to trade his way:
  1. Learn **auction market theory** and basic market mechanics (understand what drives price).
  2. Get an **order flow platform** (e.g., ATAS, DeepCharts, etc.) and use **replay mode** to get reps.
  3. **Pick ONE strategy** that complements your strengths/weaknesses — and **stop hopping.**
  4. Focus on the **execution** of that one strategy; don't stack conflicting strategies.

## 12. Key Takeaways

1. **Entries are the final 5–10%.** Context (environment + location) is the real edge.
2. **The market is an auction.** Trade the **failure of forced participation** (traps), not breakouts by default.
3. **Gamma regime sets expectations** for volatility, not direction: positive = dampening (failed breakouts), negative = amplification (fast moves).
4. **Location = where to do business:** discount vs. premium, value area, and a fib golden pocket (0.705 / 0.788 / 0.886) that must sit **outside value area**.
5. **Confirmation = absorption in discount + a "try-fail / try-again-fail" shift of dominance**, read via footprint candles (volume + delta, 400% imbalances).
6. **Stop goes beyond the failed sellers; targets are swing points** (~1.5–2R), with trailing into clusters.
7. **Real-time rule: effort vs. result.** Aggression without price progression is a red flag.
8. **Hard filters matter:** ~20,000 contracts per 5-min candle, and only the first ~1.5 hrs of the open.
9. **~60–65% win rate at ~1.5R and ~1.8 profit factor** is enough — especially with prop-firm drawdown math.
10. **Consistency comes from eliminating bad losses** (back-end optimization), not from more knowledge, more setups, or more A-game sessions.

## 13. Glossary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Order flow** | Reading bid/ask transactions (footprint) rather than only the OHLC candle. |
| **GEX / Gamma Exposure** | Net gamma of options dealers; drives their hedging flows in futures. |
| **Naive GEX** | Broad-assumption gamma estimate (vs. model-based "inferred GEX"). |
| **Call wall / Put wall** | Strike levels with large gamma concentration acting as magnets/barriers. |
| **Gamma flip zone** | Price level separating positive- and negative-gamma territory. |
| **Value area** | Price range where the most volume traded (accepted value). |
| **Discount / Premium** | Below / above the value area. |
| **POC (Point of Control)** | Price with the highest traded volume in a profile. |
| **Absorption** | Aggressive orders being filled without moving price. |
| **Shift of dominance** | Change in which side controls price progression. |
| **Delta** | Aggressive buy volume minus aggressive sell volume. |
| **Footprint candle** | Candle showing volume/delta at each price level. |
| **Low-volume node (LVN)** | Price area with little traded business; a likely revisit zone. |
| **Inefficient move** | A fast move that didn't spend enough time to build business. |
| **R (R-multiple)** | Profit/loss expressed as a multiple of the risked amount. |
| **Prop firm** | Proprietary trading firm providing funded/simulated accounts. |
| **MNQ / NQ** | Micro / full-size Nasdaq-100 E-mini futures. |
| **Auction Market Theory** | Framework treating markets as a two-way auction seeking value. |
